## Who's in the room, and what each needs to hear

ScienceClaw judges, Area A·03 Manufacturing & Materials,, with a venture fund in the audience. Three listeners, one deck:

| **Manufacturing & materials judges** | Is this a real shop-floor problem? | Slides 2, 6 | "No printed part matches its file, and the error belongs to your machine today." Hold up the plate. |
| **Investors** | Who pays, and does it get better as it grows? | Slides 2, 7 | "The value grows with every lab that joins, and the buyers are medical and bio labs that can't pool data." |

Rules for this room:
- **Say "simulation" every time a number is a simulation number.** Science judges will check; investors will remember if you didn't.
- **Lead with the problem and the buyer, not the agents.** The agent layer adds nothing measurable yet; it's a Q&A answer, not a headline.
- **Don't invent market numbers.** If asked about market size, name the buyers and their constraint; offer to follow up.
- **Q&A by listener:** science judges tend to ask "is the simulation circular?", "does this beat a single agent?" and "did you test on real printers?"; manufacturing judges ask "how do you measure?" and "Formlabs already has Fit Tuning"; investors ask "who pays?" and "how many sites do you need?". All are answered below.

## Script (~420 words spoken, ~3:00)

**1 · Title (0:00).** Hold up the printed plate. "Prebuilt: precision printing for labs that can't share their data."

**2 · Problem (0:00–0:25).** "Hospitals, dental labs and bio labs print parts that have to fit: surgical guides and crowns from patient scans, microfluidic chips with sub-millimetre channels. But no printed part matches its file. Holes come out small, pins come out fat, by tens to hundreds of microns, and each machine is wrong in its own way. You learn it by printing, every lab does that from scratch, and they can't pool what they learn: patient scans and process recipes can't leave. So we asked: can printers learn from each other without sharing a single measurement?"

**3 · How it works (0:25–0:50).** "Each lab prints this test plate and measures it. It fits a physics model of its own machine, locally. The only thing that leaves is the mean and spread of three parameters every printer shares. It's schema-checked, written on a receipt, and a privacy audit replays every crossing: zero measurements crossed."

**4 · Three rounds (0:50–1:10).** Point, don't walk through it. "Left: our network, orange, beats learning alone every round and matches pooling all the raw data. Right: one site lies. It costs one round, then trust cuts it off, and a consistency check removes even that round."

**5 · Results (1:10–1:40).** "Across a hundred simulated worlds: twelve percent lower error than learning alone, as good as pooling the data. With eight data-poor labs, thirty-five percent, in every world. A consistency check catches a liar's notes on arrival and never dropped an honest one. And what a lab actually gets is a corrected design that misses by 9 microns instead of 132. The sharing does the work, not the agents: our agent layer adds nothing measurable yet, and we say so."

**6 · Rigor (1:40–2:05).** "We printed this on a Formlabs Form Studio 1. Its smallest pin should be 1.5 millimeters; by micrometer it's 1.44, inside Formlabs' spec, and inside our simulator's range at its small edge: exactly why each printer has to learn its own error. But the two-site gain is under two microns, so only a simulation with known truth can score it, and we registered that before printing. And the simulator doesn't hand us the result: when printers share no physics, sharing gains nothing."

**7 · Who it's for (2:05–2:45).** "Who needs this: hospital labs printing surgical guides, dental labs, microfluidics and bio labs, and device makers spread across contract printers. Each has many printers, little data per printer, and data that legally or commercially can't be pooled. That's exactly where sharing pays, and the value grows with every lab that joins. Bioprinting is where we're headed; today it's direction, not a result."

**8 · Close (2:45–3:00).** "Labs learn their printers together, and their data stays home. Print anywhere, trust the part." Stop talking.

## The five points to land

1. **Precision is a property of your machine, and you only learn it by printing.** A real Form Studio 1 pin came out 24–89 µm small.
2. **The constraint is real and the design honours it:** no raw data crosses, enforced by a schema and proven by a replayable receipt chain.
3. **In simulation, sharing matches pooling the data** (12.3% vs 12.2% at round 3, 100 worlds) and grows with many data-poor sites (35% at eight, 20 of 20 worlds).
4. **Robust:** a lying site is cut to 1% trust after one round, and with the consistency check (an added protocol option, off in the registered config) its notes are caught on arrival: 180 of 180, 0 of 540 honest notes dropped, no round-1 damage (60 worlds, simulation).
5. **We only claim what can be measured.** Every result is simulated, against printers with known truth. The real printer shows the simulator is the right size; the two-site sharing gain stays a simulation result, by a prediction registered before printing.

## Likely questions, honest answers

- **"Does this beat a single agent?"** Depends which single. Beats one site learning alone: yes, 12% at two sites (10–15% across the data regimes we tried), 35% at eight (simulation). Versus pooling all the raw data in one model: matches it at two sites; at eight it does better, partly because the pooled model is too big to fit well, so say that caveat. Versus one agent per site instead of several: no evidence it's better. With messages cut, learned credibility ties equal votes (2.4%, p=0.27), a saboteur agent barely hurts, and the agents' piece choices don't beat picking pieces at random (1%, 31 of 60 worlds); the network gain survives with random pieces. The win is many sites, not many agents.
- **"Is a couple of microns worth it?"** At two sites, no, and we said so before printing: 1.6 µm, below what you can measure on a part. It grows with the number of data-poor sites (slide 6: 11%, 28%, 35% at 2, 4, 8). Two printers is the smallest possible network; the point is the method, its guarantees, and knowing when it pays. Source: `hpc/sweeps/relevance/README.md`, `hpc/sweeps/coldstart/README.md`.
- **"Did you test it on a real printer?"** One Formlabs Form Studio 1 (Alpha Fast resin), no scanner. We measured pins by micrometer: the 1.5 mm pin read 1.411 and 1.476 mm (−89 and −24 µm). Formlabs' spec is ±0.08 mm on 1–30 mm features. That checks the simulator's scale. We did not fit the model on the real printer (three pieces aren't enough), and we don't claim the sharing gain there.
- **"Then why trust the simulator?"** Its printers miss by the amounts real ones do (vendor spec, our pin readings, published small-hole errors on Formlabs printers), and every result is paired per world against a known truth. Real-site validation is the next step; the plate, hand-measurement sheet and scanner pipeline are built for it.
- **"Formlabs already has Fit Tuning."** That's a manual setting, adjusted by trial prints on one machine. Prebuilt learns each printer's error with an uncertainty from one test plate, and lets machines learn from each other without sharing measurements.
- **"How do you measure without a scanner?"** Micrometer for pins, pin gauges or calipers for holes, by hand. With no tools at all, a pin tried in a ladder of holes measures the fit to one 0.1 mm step.
- **"Couldn't you just share the data anonymized?"** Measurements of a customer's part geometry are the IP. Summaries of three process-agnostic parameters are not, and the receipts prove nothing else left.
- **"Why not start trust lower?"** Starting at 0.3 only trims the first-round damage (by about a fifth, in our trust sweep) and costs a little when everyone is honest. The consistency check does better: each receiver compares an incoming summary with its own local fit and drops it if they disagree beyond what the model allows (chi-square, 0.999). In 60 simulated worlds it caught all 180 lying notes, dropped none of 540 honest ones, and the site being lied to stayed at 11.6 µm in round 1 (64 µm without the check, 11.6 with no notes). It's an added protocol option, off in the frozen/registered config, and this liar is easy to catch (3 prior std off). A subtle liar (0.5–1 std) gets past the check a quarter to half the time and costs 11–17% in round 1. Adding adaptive spread (each receiver weights a sender by how much they have agreed so far) caps the round-1 cost at 5% for every liar we tried, against 72–534% with no defence. Also an added option, 60 worlds, `hpc/sweeps/relevance/README.md` section 8.
- **"Isn't your simulation built to confirm itself?"** We ran a negative control: the same 60 worlds, but each printer's "shared" physics drawn independently, so there's nothing to learn from the other site. Sharing then gains nothing: it's 13% worse than learning alone (19 of 60 worlds better), against 10% better (44 of 60) in the normal worlds. So the gain isn't built into the scoring; it comes from physics the printers really share. The flip side, said plainly: if real printers share less than the model assumes, sharing hurts. So we built the fix: adaptive spread, where each receiver learns how far to trust a sender from how much they have agreed. With it, printers that share nothing lose nothing (−0.4%, 30 of 60), and printers that share physics keep the full 10% gain. With the consistency check on too, +1.7% and +10.3%. Added options, off in the registered config, simulation only. Real-site validation comes next.
- **"What if real printers are different from each other?"** We simulated that: eight data-poor printers, half sharing no physics with the rest (20 worlds). Plain sharing helps the matching four by 16% and hurts the other four by 15%. With the consistency check and adaptive spread, the matching ones gain 25% and the harm to the others drops to about 1% (better than plain sharing in 16 of 20 worlds, p=0.01). Honest limit: trust only partly tells the groups apart; mismatched senders are down-weighted on average, but after three rounds most mismatches are still inside what a data-poor fit allows. So: it makes sharing safe, it doesn't yet identify bad partners. With four printers (two and two) the effect is small. Simulation only, added options, `hpc/sweeps/relevance/README.md` section 10. Longer run, newer option (predictive trust, 30 worlds, round 6, scored on held-out parts the trust step never saw): each printer scores incoming notes on its own held-out parts. Matching printers end up 33% better than going alone (20% with fixed sharing); mismatched ones go from 21% worse to 7% better (20 of 30 worlds, p=0.10), so they're no longer hurt but not reliably helped. Separation is better but not clean (AUC 0.76, about 1 in 5 matched senders flagged wrongly), so never say it identifies bad printers. Line: "Checking each note against your own held-out parts lifts the matching printers to a third better than going alone, and the others are no longer hurt" (simulation, opt-in, off in the registered config; `hpc/sweeps/relevance/README.md` section 11).
- **"Does it work at micro-scale?"** We tested a plate of 0.3–1 mm features in simulation: the sharing gain holds at 7–9% (vs 9–10% on the normal plate); it doesn't grow. The same error is about five times bigger relative to the part, so it matters more there. Below about 0.5 mm our physics model stops being believable (it predicts negative hole sizes), so we present micro-scale as direction. Section 9.
- **"Who pays?"** Not validated; say it's a hypothesis. The fleet owner pays per printer (a dental lab group, a print farm, a bureau network), because first-time fit saves reprints and material. A second route is through resin and printer makers, who want their customers' parts to fit and can't collect those customers' data. Every new printer makes the network better for the others, so the price per printer can fall as it grows. Don't quote a market size you haven't checked.
- **"What does a shop actually get?"** A corrected design: the model shifts each pin and hole so it prints at the intended size. In simulation the size error falls from 132 to 9 µm (median, 60 worlds; 90th percentile 305 → 21), and the corrected design is closer in 97% of cases. Its 90% interval is calibrated (covers 81–96%), so a shop knows how far to trust it. Source: `hpc/sweeps/relevance/README.md` section 12, `model/compensate.py`.
- **"How many sites do you need?"** In simulation, with sites that start with 4 test pieces and print 2 a round: 11% at two sites (39 of 60 worlds), 28% at four (35 of 40), 35% at eight (20 of 20), after three rounds. Source: `hpc/sweeps/coldstart/README.md`, `hpc/sweeps/relevance/README.md` section 7.
